Football produces a huge volume of statistics, but numbers are most useful when they help answer a clear question. A total without context can be misleading; a statistic connected to a role, team and period can help explain what happened on the pitch.

You do not need a spreadsheet to think about football data. Start with a simple comparison, pay attention to the conditions around it and use the result as one perspective on the game rather than a final verdict.

## Ask what a number measures

Before comparing two players, find out what the statistic counts and what it leaves out. A goal total does not describe a player’s pressing, chance creation or defensive work. A passing percentage does not tell you whether the passes moved the team forward or simply kept possession safely.

The right measure depends on the question. If you want to understand a forward’s finishing, look at goals alongside chances and minutes played. If you want to compare midfield roles, consider how each player contributes to progression, retention and defending.

## Compare like with like

A player’s team, role, league and playing time all affect the numbers they produce. Comparing a regular starter with a substitute, or a forward with a defensive midfielder, may tell you more about the context than about their relative ability.

Time periods matter too. A player can change position, manager or team during a season. Keep the comparison focused on a similar period and make sure the players were asked to do broadly comparable jobs.

## Use data to guide curiosity

Statistics can point you toward an interesting question: why did a player’s involvement rise, why does a team create chances from wide areas, or how does a new role affect a midfielder’s passing? Watching matches and reading the tactical context can help explain patterns that a table alone cannot.
