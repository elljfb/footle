A player clue is most useful when it is considered alongside the rest of the board. A position may describe a huge group of footballers, but combine it with a club, league and nationality and the candidate list can become much smaller.

Footle gives feedback after each guess so that you can adjust your thinking. You do not need to recognise the answer immediately: the game is designed to let you learn something from every attempt and gradually build a clearer picture of the mystery player.

## Know what each result confirms

Green is an exact match and grey is not a match. Orange is reserved for a defined near match; it does not mean that an answer is generally similar. In Footle, orange can indicate:

- An age within three years of the mystery player’s age.
- A height within five centimetres of the mystery player’s height.
- A different nationality from the same continent.
- A different club in the same league.
- A different sub-position within the same broad position.

Position, league and preferred foot have to match exactly to show green. The sub-position clue is only close when both players share the same broad position. For age and height, the arrow points toward the answer: it indicates whether the mystery player is older or younger, or taller or shorter, than the player you guessed.

Keep exact and close results separate. If a nationality is orange, you have a continent to consider, not a confirmed country. If a club is orange, the player is associated with a different club in the same league, not the club you guessed.

## Choose an informative next guess

After each turn, ask what you still do not know and what a new guess could establish. Exact results make useful anchors. Near results set boundaries: for example, an orange age result narrows the possible ages, while an orange height result narrows the possible heights. Use the arrows to choose players on the appropriate side of the value you guessed.

Suppose a guess gives you an exact league and position, an orange club, and an orange nationality. You now know to keep looking in that league and position, but to consider another club in the competition and other countries on the same continent. The orange results do not tell you which club or country is correct, so use age, height, sub-position and preferred foot to distinguish between candidates.

A useful guess tests more than one open question. If you have narrowed the answer to a league and broad position, choose a player from that league whose age, nationality or club can help you separate the remaining candidates. Avoid spending turns on famous names chosen without a reason, or repeating a guess that would only confirm something you already know.

## Keep a shortlist and account for the player data

Write down a few players who fit the confirmed clues rather than settling on the first plausible name. Remove candidates only when a result rules them out; an orange result still leaves several possibilities. A simple shortlist helps prevent a hunch about one clue from overriding what the rest of the board says.

Footle’s player list covers players from Europe’s five major leagues, but it is a maintained dataset rather than a live roster. A player or recent transfer may not always appear, and the game can only accept names in its list. If a player is missing, use another valid guess to test the clues you still need.

Squad details and player roles can also vary between data sources. Use the result shown by the game as the reference for that puzzle instead of assuming that a player’s real-world role or recent club change will be reflected in the dataset.

## A repeatable solving routine

1. Read all eight categories after a guess: position, sub-position, age, nationality, club, league, height and preferred foot.
2. Record exact matches as anchors and note the boundaries suggested by orange results.
3. Use the age and height arrows to decide whether to test a higher or lower value.
4. Choose a valid player who can test more than one of your remaining ideas.
5. Revisit your shortlist after every result, removing only the candidates that no longer fit.

This approach turns a daily football quiz into a process of elimination rather than a series of unrelated guesses. The aim is not to know every player in advance; it is to make each attempt more informative than the last.
